> **TL;DR:** Between 14 and 29 October 2026 we sent 39 cold emails to creators and got no positive reply. Every email led with what we had built, so we had skipped the only part of selling that is about the buyer. Our rule now: before any pitch, ask three questions. How do you do this today? What about it is broken? If nothing changes for a year, what happens? When the third answer is "nothing much", there is no sale, and the right move is to say so and leave.

We counted on 29 October 2026. Thirty-nine first emails, sent over fifteen days, to creators who run courses, memberships and newsletters. One bounce. One unsubscribe. Zero positive replies. Not a single "tell me more".

The emails were not bad by the usual measures. A deliverability tester scored the sending domain 9.3 out of 10. The subject lines were plain. Each one went to a person we had actually looked at. And every one of them opened the same way: here is what we built for you, here is the link, have a look.

That was the mistake, and it took the full zero to see it. We were pitching before we had asked anything.

## What a pitch assumes

A pitch assumes three things about the person reading it. That they do this task today in a way you understand. That the way they do it hurts. And that the hurt is big enough that they will spend money and attention to make it stop.

We assumed all three. A creator with 300,000 subscribers on a video platform must surely want a site they own. Their words live on someone else's servers; that must bother them. If the platform changes its rules, they lose everything; that must keep them up at night.

Maybe. But we never asked, so we never found out, and the emails read like they were written for a person who had already agreed with us. The reader has not agreed with you. The reader has not thought about it at all. Most of them do not have the problem you are selling the cure for, or do not have it badly enough to act, and no sentence about your product changes that.

So we have changed the order. Before the product gets a sentence, three questions get asked. They are not clever. Their value is that they are asked, and asked in this sequence.

## One: how do you do this today?

The first question is about their current state, in their words. Where do your articles live now? How does a new reader find you? What do you do when someone wants to pay you?

It sounds like small talk, and that is fine, because it does three jobs at once. It tells you whether they even do the thing you help with. It gives you their vocabulary, which is the vocabulary your pitch will have to use later. And it earns the right to ask the next question, because you have shown interest in their set-up before you showed interest in replacing it.

Listen for the set-up itself, not for an opinion about it. "I post on YouTube and I have a Substack for the written stuff" is an answer. "It's fine" is not; it is a polite way of ending the conversation, and you should ask again, more specifically.

If the answer is that they do not do this at all, and have no plan to, stop here. You have learned something cheap. There is no second question, because there is nothing to be broken.

## Two: what about it is broken?

The second question is about pain. Not your idea of their pain. Theirs.

What is the part of that you would never do again if you could avoid it? When did it last cost you a day? What have you tried to fix it?

The third of those is the one we now care most about, because it separates a complaint from a problem. Someone who has tried to fix a thing has already decided it is worth fixing. Someone who merely dislikes a thing has not. Both will tell you it is annoying. Only one will pay you.

This is also where most of our assumptions would have died, quickly and in private. We believed creators were worried about owning their words. Some are. But a creator whose income arrives through a platform's payment system has a more immediate concern than ownership: not breaking the thing that pays them. If you had asked, you would have heard that in the first minute, and you would have written a different email, or none.

An honest answer to the second question is a gift, and it should be treated like one. Write it down in their words. Do not translate it into your feature list yet.

## Three: if nothing changes, what happens?

The third question is the one almost nobody asks, and it is the one that decides whether there is a sale.

Suppose you keep doing it exactly this way for another year. What does that look like? What will you have, and what will you have lost?

The point is not to frighten anyone. The point is to find out whether the pain from question two has consequences, or whether it is just friction they have learned to live with. "I'd keep losing a few hours a month" is friction. "I'd still have no way to reach my audience if the platform suspends me, and I know two people that happened to" is a consequence. The first does not buy a product. The second might.

Their answer to this question is also your pitch, if there is going to be one. You now know the state, the pain and the cost of inaction, and all three are in their words. The product gets described as the thing that changes the third answer. Nothing else about it matters to them, and nothing else should be in the email.

## What this costs, and what it buys

It costs volume. You cannot ask three questions in a cold email and get answers; you can ask one, and most people will not reply to that either. So the fast, pitch-first email we sent 39 times is replaced by something slower: a first message that asks one real question about how they work today, and a product that is not mentioned until there is an answer. Fewer sends. More conversations that go nowhere, and that end on purpose when the third answer is "nothing much".

It buys three things. You stop pitching people who were never going to buy, which is most people, and you find that out at the cost of one question instead of a sequence of follow-ups. The pitch you do make is in the buyer's words, about the buyer's consequence, which is the only kind anyone reads to the end. And you learn, from the people who answer the first question and never reach the third, what your market actually looks like, which is worth more than any of the 39 emails taught us.

We are a small team with a product we believe in, and belief is exactly what makes you skip the questions. You already know the answer, so why ask? Because you do not. Thirty-nine times zero is what knowing the answer looked like for us.

If you sell anything, try it on your next ten conversations: state, pain, consequence, in that order, and no product until the third. Keep a note of how many stop at question one. That number is your market, and it is better to learn it from ten questions than from thirty-nine silences.
